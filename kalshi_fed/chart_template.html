Method and validation

Why this method, and how it holds up

Our starting point is the Federal Reserve Board paper that first built these distributions. We reproduce its result, show where our method differs and why, and test both against the NY Fed's dealer survey.

Starting point: Diercks, Katz & Wright (2026)

Anthony Diercks, Jared Dean Katz and Jonathan Wright, Kalshi and the Rise of Macro Markets, NBER Working Paper 34702 / Federal Reserve FEDS 2026-010. They turn the same Kalshi ladders into daily distributions and find the mode was right the day before every FOMC meeting since 2022, beating fed funds futures. We follow their construction and change two steps.

StepDiercks–Katz–WrightOursWhy we changed it
Contract priceLast trade, carried forward on days without oneBid/ask midpoint if the spread is ≤20¢; last trade clipped to the quote if ≤40¢; otherwise dropped
Monotone ladderBuilt outward from the mode toward the tailsWeighted isotonic fit: the closest non-increasing curve, tighter quotes weigh moreUses every strike's information rather than trusting the mode's neighbours. Turns out to matter little once prices are current (see the ablation).
Unpriceable daysKeptFlagged when more than 10% of probability sits outside or between usable strikesMakes it visible when a number is extrapolation rather than market pricing.
Everything elseSame: price = risk-neutral probability, difference adjacent strikes, carry quiet days forward, take the mean, mode and medianKeeps the comparison to the two choices above.

Why we changed it: which choice matters

The two methods differ in two independent choices. Running all four combinations on the same meeting-days attributes the difference to each. Our quotes + isotonic cell reproduces the main series exactly. Values are mean absolute error in bp; lower is better.

Outside benchmark: NY Fed dealer survey

What this does and doesn't show

Supported

Their day-before result replicates exactly, on their window and on 2021 and 2026, which they don't cover. Beyond a month, quote-based prices are more accurate and less noisy than last trades, and Kalshi's mode matches or beats the dealer survey at every horizon.

Not yet shown

None of the gaps has been tested for statistical significance (their paper uses Diebold–Mariano tests). Forecasts on consecutive days overlap, so the effective sample is closer to the 41 meetings than to thousands of days. The benchmark doesn't yet include fed funds futures or SOFR options.

Assumptions

Their paper doesn't state the time of day for the last trade; we use the end of each ET day. The survey comparison uses the dealer panel on each survey's due date, and skips the November 2022 to June 2023 surveys, which are published only as images.

From contracts to a rate

Each contract pays $1 if the upper bound is above strike k. Its price is P(rate > k). Differencing across the ladder gives the probability of each 25bp outcome, and the expected upper bound is the probability-weighted sum.

Pricing a contract each day

Close bid/ask midpoint when the spread is 20¢ or less. Up to 40¢, the last trade clipped to the quote. Wider than that, the strike is dropped. Quotes carry forward on days with no candle. A weighted isotonic fit (tighter quotes weigh more) makes probabilities non-increasing in strike.

Effective rate basis

Kalshi settles on the target's upper bound; fed funds futures settle on the effective rate (EFFR), which trades inside the range. The EFFR-basis figure adds the median EFFR minus upper-bound gap over the last 20 days, from FRED: about −17bp through 2024, about −12bp in 2026.

Reliability

A meeting-day is flagged unreliable when more than 10% of probability sits beyond the usable strikes (as in 2022, when hikes outran the ladder) or in gaps wider than 50bp between them. Those days are drawn as gray dashes in the explorer and left out of the history lines.
